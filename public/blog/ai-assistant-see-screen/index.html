AI Assistant That Can See Your Screen: 4 Options Compared

November 23, 2026·7 min read

Quick answer

Several AI assistants can see your screen now, but they look in different ways and stop at different points. Copilot Vision on Windows watches what you share and explains it, but will not click or type. ChatGPT's desktop app can grab a snapshot of one window. Gemini sees the page in Chrome, or your whole phone screen in Gemini Live. Filect, on Windows and Mac, looks at your screen when you ask, answers out loud, and can then do the task. Pick by whether you need an explanation or the task done.

"Just look at this" is the most natural thing to say to someone helping you with a computer. Describing an error or a settings page in words is slow, and you usually leave out the detail that matters. An assistant that can see your screen skips that step.

The trouble is that "sees your screen" covers very different features. This guide compares the four options you are most likely to find, plus the privacy questions to ask first.

Three ways an assistant can "see" your screen

Know which kind of seeing you are getting, because it changes what the assistant can help with.

  • A live share. You start a session and pick a window or your desktop. The assistant follows along as you scroll and click, and you can talk about it as it changes. It stops when you end the share.
  • A snapshot. You send one capture of a window into a chat. Good for a single question, but it does not follow you to the next screen.
  • The page only. A browser assistant reads the web page you are on. Useful for articles and web apps, but blind to your desktop apps, files and system dialogs.

The second question: once it sees the problem, can it do anything about it, or only explain?

Copilot Vision: the built in Windows option

If you are on Windows 11, Copilot Vision is the first thing to try. You start a voice chat in the Copilot app, then choose a window or your desktop to share. Microsoft says you can share up to two apps at a time. When you select Stop on the floating toolbar, Copilot stops seeing the content, but the voice chat keeps going.

It is good at explaining: where a tool is, what a setting does, what a document says. Microsoft is clear about the limit, though. Vision guides you, and it may highlight part of the screen, but it does not click, type or scroll for you.

Access is the other catch. Microsoft's support pages say Vision needs a Microsoft 365 Personal, Family or Premium subscription, or a work Copilot license.

Pick it if you are on Windows, already pay for Microsoft 365, and mostly want things explained.

ChatGPT desktop app: a snapshot, not a live view

The ChatGPT desktop app for Windows and Mac takes the snapshot approach. OpenAI's documentation describes Appshots, which send the window in front to a chat, including an image of it and the text the app exposes. OpenAI advises leaving sensitive content out unless the task needs it.

This is handy for one question about one window. It is not a running conversation about your screen: OpenAI's voice help page says Live voice does not support video or screen sharing. OpenAI also documents a separate Computer Use feature that can work in desktop apps you allow, so check which features your plan includes.

Pick it if you already live in ChatGPT and want to drop a window into a typed chat.

Gemini: your browser tab, or your phone

Google splits this by device. On a computer, Gemini in Chrome uses the tab you are viewing by default, and a "Select from screen" option lets you draw a box around part of a page before you ask. Its view stops at the browser, so a desktop app or a Windows dialog is out of reach.

On a phone, Gemini Live can share your full screen while you talk. Google says screen sharing in Live is free on Android and iOS. Sharing ends if the screen locks or Live is put on hold, and you have to share again.

Pick it if your question is about a web page, or you want screen help on your phone.

Things worth asking an assistant that can see

Screen awareness earns its keep on tasks that are painful to describe. A few that work well with most of the tools above (Gemini in Chrome only for web pages):

  1. Error messages. "What does this mean and what should I try first?" saves you retyping a long error code.
  2. Unfamiliar settings. "Which of these do I need to change to stop the laptop sleeping?" on a crowded settings page.
  3. Spreadsheets. "Why is this formula showing an error?" while the cell is in view.
  4. Forms and documents. "Summarize what this page is asking me to agree to" before you click Accept.

With an assistant that can act, you can go one step further and finish the job: "rename these files to match the dates in them" or "draft a reply to this email saying Thursday works". Our guide to an AI that controls your computer goes deeper on that side.

Privacy: questions to ask before you let it look

Your screen shows more than you think: notifications, open tabs, names in a chat. Check four things first.

  • When does it look? Only during a share, on request, or over time? Check its settings for anything that captures your screen in the background, and learn the indicator that shows when it is looking.
  • What exactly is shared? One app window is less exposure than your whole desktop. Copilot Vision lets you pick apps; Gemini in Chrome lets you select part of a page.
  • How do you stop it? Know the stop button before you need it.
  • What should never be on screen? Close password managers, banking pages and private messages before you share, whatever tool you use.

  • "I want to drop a window into a chat I already use." ChatGPT's desktop app.

The bottom line

The real differences between these assistants are how they look (a live share, a snapshot or just the browser tab) and whether they stop at an explanation. Copilot Vision, Gemini and ChatGPT are good ways to get things explained. If you want to say "look at this and fix it" and have it happen, pick an assistant that can act, and keep approvals on until you trust it.
