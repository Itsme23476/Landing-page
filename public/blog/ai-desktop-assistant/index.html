AI Desktop Assistant: The 5 Types and How to Choose

November 14, 2026·8 min read

Quick answer

An AI desktop assistant is an AI you run as an app on your Windows PC or Mac instead of in a browser tab. There are five types: chatbot apps, assistants built into the operating system, AI launchers, voice command tools, and agent driven assistants. If you mostly want answers and writing help, a chatbot app is enough. If you want the assistant to find your files and finish tasks in your apps, pick an agent driven one and test it on copies of your files first.

Most people meet AI in a browser tab. You open a chat site, paste something in, copy the answer out, and do the rest yourself. A desktop assistant promises to close that gap: it sits on your computer, opens with a shortcut or your voice, and is close enough to your work to help with it directly.

The trouble is that "AI desktop assistant" now describes very different products. Some are the same chatbot in a window. Some can read your screen. A few can open your files, run commands, and send the email for you. This guide sorts them into five types, the questions that separate them, and a twenty minute test to run before you commit.

What makes it a desktop assistant

Being installed is not the interesting part. What a desktop app can do that a browser tab cannot comes down to four things:

  • It is always one shortcut away. A global hotkey or a voice trigger works from any window, so you do not switch context to ask.
  • It can see what you are working on. Some assistants can look at your screen or the selected text when you ask them to.
  • It can reach your files. Your documents live on your disk, not in a chat history. An assistant on the desktop can index and open them, if it is built to.
  • It can act. The most capable ones run commands, edit files, and work inside connected apps such as your email and calendar.

An app that does only the first is a convenient chatbot. One that does all four is a real assistant.

The 5 types of AI desktop assistant

1. Chatbot desktop apps

OpenAI and Anthropic both offer desktop apps for ChatGPT and Claude on Windows and Mac. You get the same models as on the web, opened from a keyboard shortcut, with the ability to drop in files and screenshots. Claude's desktop app can also connect to local tools through the Model Context Protocol, which lets it reach things like a folder on your machine once you set that up.

Good at: writing, explaining, summarizing, brainstorming. Limited at: doing things. Out of the box, you still carry the answer back to your files and apps yourself.

2. Assistants built into the operating system

On Windows 11, Microsoft's Copilot app is the built in option. Microsoft has announced an opt in "Hey Copilot" wake word and an experimental agent feature called Copilot Actions that attempts tasks in desktop and web apps, which started as a preview for Windows Insiders. Apple builds Siri into macOS for quick system requests.

Good at: quick questions and help with nothing to install. Limited at: multi step work on your own files, at least until the agent features reach your build. We walk through the Windows options in detail in our guide to Jarvis for PC.

3. AI launchers

Launchers such as Raycast on the Mac add an AI chat to the command bar you already use to open apps. You select some text, hit a shortcut, and ask for a rewrite or a summary without leaving the window.

Good at: fast, keyboard driven micro tasks. Limited at: anything longer than a prompt and a reply.

4. Voice command tools

Windows Voice access and macOS Voice Control are free, built in, and let you operate the computer by speaking: "open Word," "click File," "scroll down." They are excellent for accessibility, but you still decide every step. You say the click, and it clicks.

5. Agent driven assistants

The newest type takes a goal instead of a command. You say "find the March invoice from the electrician and email it to my accountant," and the assistant works out the steps: search, open, attach, draft. Under the hood, an AI agent reads and writes files, runs commands, and uses connections to your apps. Filect is in this group, along with a number of open source projects. They do the most, which is exactly why they need the most careful testing.

Seven questions that separate them

Feature lists blur together. These questions do not.

  1. How do you call it? A hotkey, push to talk, or a wake word. Push to talk captures nothing until you hold a key. A wake word is more hands free but means a detector is listening while the app runs.
  2. Can it see your screen, and only when you ask? Useful for "what does this error mean?" You want it on request, not constantly.
  3. Does it know what is inside your files? Many assistants can open a file you hand them. Far fewer can find one when you only describe it, because that needs an index of the contents, not just the filenames.
  4. Can it act in your apps? Check the list of connections. If your email, calendar, or notes app is not on it, the assistant cannot work there.
  5. Does it ask before acting? Look for a mode where each action waits for your approval, plus a visible record of every step.
  6. What does it send to the cloud? Your audio, your screen, and your file contents. Each tool should say plainly what leaves your machine and whether it is stored.
  7. How is it priced? Flat subscription, per use, or running on an AI plan you already pay for. Heavy use on a per task model adds up fast.

A twenty minute test before you commit

Demos are recorded on the happy path. Run these five checks on any assistant during its trial.

  1. The shortcut test (2 minutes). Call it from three different apps, including one in full screen. If it does not come up reliably, you will stop using it within a week.
  2. The forgotten file test (5 minutes). Ask for a document by describing what is in it, not its name: "the lease with the pet clause." This tells you whether it reads contents or only filenames. Our guide to searching files by content explains why this is harder than it sounds.
  3. The screen test (3 minutes). Open an error message or a dense chart and ask what it means. Check that it only looked when you asked.
  4. The draft test (5 minutes). In a connected email or calendar app, ask it to draft something rather than send it. Open the draft and judge it.
  5. The delete test (5 minutes). In a folder of copied test files, ask it to delete two of them. A good assistant asks first. If it just does it, keep approvals on or keep looking.

The bottom line

The best AI desktop assistant is the one that matches the job you want done. For writing and answers, a chatbot app is fast and familiar. For quick help with nothing to install, start with what your operating system already includes. For an assistant that finds your files and finishes tasks in your apps, choose an agent driven one, keep approvals on while you learn it, and test it on copies first. FAQ

What is an AI desktop assistant?

An AI desktop assistant is an AI that runs as an app on your Windows PC or Mac rather than in a browser tab. It opens from a shortcut or your voice, and depending on the type it can see your screen when asked, find your files, and take actions in your apps.

What is the best AI desktop assistant?

It depends on the job. Chatbot apps like ChatGPT and Claude are best for writing and answers. Built in options like Copilot on Windows need nothing extra installed. Agent driven assistants such as Filect are best when you want the assistant to find files and complete multi step tasks in your apps by voice.

Is there a free AI desktop assistant?

Yes. Windows 11 includes Copilot and Voice access, and macOS includes Siri and Voice Control. ChatGPT and Claude also have free tiers in their desktop apps. Assistants that act on your files and apps are usually paid, though many offer a free trial.

Can an AI desktop assistant work by voice on Windows?

Yes. Voice access and Copilot both take spoken input on Windows 11. Filect takes spoken requests on Windows and Mac and turns them into finished tasks, from finding a file to drafting an email in a connected app.
